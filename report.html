Abstract We evaluate TypeSafe's Jev (jev-latest), a non-generative “System One” classification model, on 6,021 multiple-choice questions spanning the WMDP benchmark (bio, chem, cyber, and a shortcut-robust bio variant) and six text-based subtasks of LAB-Bench. Each item is posed as a single typed choice decision against Jev's HTTP API, with the model's returned option and per-option probability distribution scored against ground truth. Accuracy ranges from 85.2% on WMDP-Bio recall questions down to 36.6% on LAB-Bench SuppQA. We identify two distinct failure regimes: honest, low-confidence guessing on tasks requiring multi-step computation or derivation (WMDP-Cyber, LAB-Bench), accompanied by a measurable option-order position bias; and confident, miscalibrated errors on knowledge questions with closely matched distractors (WMDP-Bio/Chem). Pooled calibration analysis (ECE = 0.091) shows Jev's confidence score is a usable, if imperfect, signal for confidence-gated escalation to a stronger fallback model.

1Introduction

The Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) benchmark measures hazardous knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security domains as a proxy for evaluating and unlearning dangerous capabilities in language models. LAB-Bench evaluates practical biology-research capabilities — cloning design, protocol troubleshooting, sequence manipulation, literature and database lookup — directly relevant to dual-use biosecurity risk assessment. This report describes a pipeline built to evaluate TypeSafe's Jev model, a proprietary “System One” model that returns typed, probabilistic decisions in a single parallel pass rather than generating free text, against both benchmarks, and analyzes where and why its answers diverge from ground truth.

2Method

Model interface. Each question is submitted to POST api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone with model=jev-latest. The question stem and enumerated options (A, B, C…) are placed in the state field as plain text; a single choice-type typed question is attached whose criteria map each option letter to its literal text. Jev returns a selected letter, a full probability distribution over letters, and a scalar confidence.

Scoring. The returned letter is mapped back to its original option index and compared against the dataset's ground-truth answer index. Requests are issued concurrently (16 workers) with exponential backoff on HTTP 429/529.

Dataset preparation. WMDP subsets and WMDP-Bio-Robust were pulled directly from their published Hugging Face parquet files; WMDP-Bio-Robust's choices field (a Python/NumPy array repr mixing single- and double-quoted strings) required a quote-aware regex tokenizer, validated on all 811 rows. LAB-Bench subtasks store an ideal string plus a distractors list rather than a fixed index; options were deduplicated and shuffled with a fixed seed before submission. Two LAB-Bench subtasks, FigQA and TableQA, encode their supporting evidence as embedded raster images rather than text; since Jev's state field accepts only text or structured text/JSON, these were excluded rather than scored on question text alone.

5Discussion

Two qualitatively distinct failure modes emerge. Derivation tasks: WMDP-Cyber questions requiring multi-step arithmetic or code/assembly tracing, and the majority of LAB-Bench items, are answered with markedly lower average confidence when wrong than when right — Jev is aware it is guessing. Re-submitting a sample of 15 originally-wrong WMDP-Cyber questions with options reshuffled flipped 4/15 (27%) to correct purely by removing the position bias in Figure 3, confirming some of this error mass is an architecture artifact rather than a knowledge gap. Knowledge-precision tasks: WMDP-Bio/Chem questions with closely matched, deliberately confusable distractors instead produce a meaningful share of confidently wrong answers. The same reshuffling intervention had almost no effect here (0/15 bio, 1/15 chem flipped) — these are genuine knowledge/grounding failures, and would need retrieval-augmented context rather than ensembling to fix.

Because Jev's confidence is directionally informative but not perfectly calibrated, it is best used as a routing signal rather than a correctness guarantee: escalating the lowest-confidence tail of answers to a slower, generative fallback model is projected to raise WMDP-Cyber accuracy from 63.4% to roughly 86% if 64% of items are escalated to an 85%-accurate fallback — at a fraction of the cost of routing every question to that fallback.

6Limitations

(i) FigQA and TableQA were excluded outright — a coverage gap, not a measured failure. (ii) Jev is a closed, proprietary model with unknown training data and cutoff, so accuracy on any public benchmark may be inflated by memorization; the WMDP-Bio vs. WMDP-Bio-Robust gap (85.2% → 80.2%) is suggestive of this but not conclusive. (iii) Each question was submitted once; estimates would benefit from repeated sampling or explicit option-order ensembling at scale. (iv) One CloningScenarios item (~15K characters) exceeded Jev's input token limit and was scored as an error.

7Conclusion

Jev performs well above chance on every evaluated benchmark but trails what a full reasoning LLM would be expected to score, particularly on derivation-heavy and fine-grained-retrieval tasks. Its calibrated-enough confidence signal makes it a plausible fast first-pass filter ahead of a stronger fallback model in a biosecurity-relevant screening pipeline, provided the routing threshold accounts for its systematically different error profile on knowledge-recall versus derivation tasks.
